# Musina in Motion — 8-minute mapping interface script

**Target:** 8–9 minutes at a measured pace  
**Speaker role:** mapping/spatial interface section of a three-person presentation  
**Core thread:** *Where is somebody from — and when?*  
**Recurring idea:** geography does not merely illustrate the analysis; it changes the questions we can ask of it.

---

## Opening — landing page

**[ON SCREEN: Musina in Motion landing page]**

I want to begin with a question that most of us ask without thinking very much about it: **where are you from?** It sounds like a request for one place — a town, a country, perhaps the place where somebody was born — and survey research often treats it in exactly that way. We ask the question, record the answer, turn it into a variable and move on. But Musina kept resisting that simplicity.

A person can be born in Zimbabwe, spend time living or working somewhere else in South Africa, arrive in Musina from Johannesburg, cross the border repeatedly, return home for family responsibilities, and still be building a life in Musina. All of those geographies can be true at the same time. Once we began looking at the data spatially, the more useful question became: **from where, at what point in somebody's life, and through which places?**

That is the idea behind **Musina in Motion**. We call it an atlas because no single map can carry the whole argument. The atlas lets us move between scales and forms of evidence — regional connections, the local geography of the survey, settlement and circulation, institutional services, the resources people themselves named, and finally the journeys narrated in the interviews. The point is not simply to put data on maps; it is to ask what becomes visible when geography becomes part of the analysis.

That also explains the metaphor of Musina as a **regional mobility hinge**. A hinge is small relative to the spaces it connects, but movement depends on it. Musina sits at one national border, yet the mobility systems meeting there extend well beyond that border and also turn back into South Africa itself. People arrive, leave, return, circulate, settle temporarily and settle more permanently. Musina is therefore not just the end of a journey; it is one of the places through which different journeys are organised.

---

## Regional geography — birthplace and recent origin

**[CLICK: Survey Atlas → Regional field]**

The first thing we did was separate two variables that are easy to collapse: **birthplace** and **recent origin**. Birthplace records where a person was born. Recent origin records the last migration link before Musina. They sound similar, but they are asking different temporal questions.

**[TOGGLE: Birthplace]**

When we map birthplace, Zimbabwe dominates the survey geography: **75.5%** of the analytical sample were born in Zimbabwe, while **17.3%** were born in South Africa. That gives us one regional picture.

**[TOGGLE: Recent origin]**

But when we change the definition to the most recent place from which somebody moved before Musina, the geography shifts. Zimbabwe remains dominant at **71.7%**, but South Africa rises to **23.8%**. The same participants produce a different map because we changed the temporal meaning of the word *origin*.

That difference is easy to miss in a large table. On a map its implication is much easier to grasp: some people arriving in Musina are not moving directly from another country into the town. Their recent mobility history includes places inside South Africa. So the regional field is not simply a set of international origin countries; it also contains internal circulation and multi-stage movement.

This is one of the first things geography gave us that a conventional descriptive table does not communicate nearly as intuitively. The map did not invent a new finding; it made the structure of an existing finding visible.

## Local atlas — where the survey met Musina

**[SCROLL: Local Atlas]**

Then we come down in scale. The regional map asks how Musina is connected to other places; the local atlas asks **where the survey actually encountered these mobility experiences inside Musina**.

The coloured circles are broad survey block anchors, not households and not individual respondents. They locate the fieldwork across farm, trade, township and residential contexts while deliberately keeping respondent-level locations off the map. That privacy decision is analytically important. Spatial analysis gives us the technical capacity to become very precise, but more geographic precision is not automatically more truth — and with migration data it can also become more risk.

Once the blocks and wards are visible together, another simplification starts to fall apart: the idea that there is one Musina mobility experience.

---

## Ward configurations — mobile where, and in what way?

**[SCROLL: Ward ecology]**

Take Ward 2, for example. In this surveyed ward configuration, **64.5%** of participants are non-permanent, **84.1%** were born in Zimbabwe, and **81.3%** report crossing the border at least yearly. In Ward 3, the corresponding figures are **44% non-permanent**, **66% Zimbabwe-born**, and **49% crossing at least yearly**.

I do not want to turn those numbers into labels for whole communities. The useful point is that places contain **different combinations of settlement and circulation**. Permanence, birthplace, cross-border movement and the local fieldwork context vary together in different ways. The spatial question therefore becomes: **mobile where, and in what way?**

A town-wide average can tell us something important about the sample, but it can also flatten local configurations. The map gives us a way to keep the aggregate pattern while still asking where it is stronger, weaker or combined differently.

## Service context — proximity is not access

**[SCROLL: Infrastructure]**

Once people are somewhere, another set of questions becomes spatial: what is around them, what can they reach, and what does proximity actually mean?

We map selected institutional and mobility anchors — schools, health services, police, transport and border infrastructure — because these places form part of the geography people move through. But this is also where GIS can tempt us to overclaim. A clinic being nearby does not mean somebody can use it. There may be transport costs, opening hours, documentation requirements, waiting times, language barriers, safety concerns, discrimination or poor continuity of care.

So we use a simple discipline: **near is not the same as accessible**. GIS can measure spatial separation from an eligible facility class. Effective access is a longer chain: somebody must recognise a need, seek help, reach the service, be able to enter or use it, and continue care. That distinction matters particularly in a border setting where people's service geographies may not stop at one jurisdiction.

## Participatory resources — what can GIS see?

**[SCROLL: Participatory resource ecology]**

The participatory mapping gave us one of the most interesting methodological lessons in the whole exercise. Across the workshop material, participants named **128 unique resources**. **Thirty-nine** had usable coordinates, and only **37** entered the core mapped layer.

At first, that can look like a mundane data-cleaning problem. But when we compared categories, the loss was not random. Education and youth resources were relatively easy to map. State and legal support were also much more visible. Faith and social support, community places, livelihoods and basic services were far less likely to become stable points.

That means the map has a **visibility structure**. GIS is very good at seeing things that have buildings, names, addresses, institutional records and stable coordinates. But people often navigate cities through resources that do not look like that: relationships, temporary places, local knowledge, informal livelihood spaces, social networks and services that move.

So one of the most useful questions the resource map taught us to ask is: **am I showing you the resource system, or the part of the resource system that GIS can see?** The unmapped part is not simply missing data. Its pattern tells us something about the limits of the method itself.

## Narrated Journeys — putting time back into the map

**[CLICK: Narrated Journeys]**

The survey atlas shows patterns, but patterns compress lives. The interview journeys allow us to put **time back into geography**.

These ten maps are interpretive journey reconstructions, not GPS traces. The places are geographically anchored, and the route lines connect the narrated stops so that we can follow sequence without pretending that we know the exact road travelled.

**[OPEN: Rhoda]**

Rhoda's journey makes this very clear. She moves from Gokwe to Bulawayo intending to reach Cape Town, where her uncle lives. Instead, the person she paid to take her leaves her in Musina. She later moves to Tzaneen for work and then returns to Musina.

If we reduced that account to **Zimbabwe → South Africa**, almost everything analytically interesting would disappear: the intended destination, the interrupted journey, the period in a shelter, the work move to Tzaneen, the return to Musina, and the way the meaning of Musina changes over time. It begins as an unexpected stopping place and later becomes a place to which she returns.

That is what the scrollytelling is doing. As the narrative advances, the map changes with it, but the wider trajectory remains visible. We are not asking the interview to represent the survey; we are using it to recover sequence, contingency and changing relationships to place.

---

## Closing — what did mapping add?

**[PAUSE on journey map or return to landing page]**

If I had to summarise the contribution of the mapping work, I would return to the question we began with: **where is somebody from?** Musina shows us that the answer depends on when we ask, what kind of movement we mean, and what scale of geography we are looking at.

The survey tells us how patterns are distributed. Spatial analysis shows where those patterns sit in relation to one another. Participatory mapping reveals the resource landscape people recognise in practice — and, importantly, the parts that formal GIS struggles to see. The interviews show how movement unfolds through time, interruption, work, waiting, return and settlement. None of these methods replaces the others; together they let us ask better questions of the same mobility system.

For a project that has invested heavily in comparable research across different sites, I think that matters. The return is not simply a collection of datasets, tables or individual maps. It is a reusable **evidence system**: a way of moving between scale, method and question while keeping clear what each source can and cannot establish. That is what I hope this atlas demonstrates. Geography has not simply illustrated the Musina research; it has helped us see structure in the evidence that would otherwise be much easier to miss.
